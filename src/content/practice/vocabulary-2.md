This set tests how words work in real sentences. You need to read the whole sentence, not just the word.

## Words in context

When you do not know a word, look for clues around it. In "The well was *parched*; not a drop of water was left", the second half tells you parched means very dry. Clues often come after a comma, a semicolon or words like *because*, *so* and *but*.

## Homophones and homonyms

| Kind | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Homophones | sound the same, spelt differently, different meanings | *right* and *write*, *flour* and *flower*, *weak* and *week* |
| Homonyms | one spelling, more than one meaning | *bat* (a cricket bat, the animal), *match* (a cricket match, a matchstick) |

To choose between homophones, ask what job the word does in the sentence, then check its spelling against that meaning.

## One-word substitutes

These are single words that replace a long phrase. Roots help a lot:

- *auto* = self (autobiography, automatic)
- *bio* = life (biology, biography)
- *uni-* = one (uniform, unicycle)
- *aqua* = water (aquarium)

## Idioms

An idiom is a phrase whose meaning is different from its words. *Let the cat out of the bag* means to tell a secret by mistake; no cat is involved. Learn idioms as whole phrases. In a question, the option that takes the words literally is almost always a trap.

## Worked example

*"Arjun visits his cousins in Kochi once in a blue moon." Once in a blue moon means: every month, very rarely, every full moon, only at night.*

Moons are not really blue, so the literal options are traps. The idiom means **very rarely**.
