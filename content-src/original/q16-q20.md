## Q16. LLM-as-a-judge: how to make it trustworthy
### Short answer
A judge is a measurement instrument, so I treat it like one. I calibrate it against human labels, measure its agreement and bias, and only trust it in the regime where I've validated it. Concretely, that means a narrow rubric that forces a decision, pairwise or binary verdicts instead of 1-10 scores, position-swapping and length controls, and a human-labeled golden set of a few hundred items. On that set the judge has to hit roughly human-human agreement (Cohen's kappa of about 0.6-0.8, or above 80% raw agreement on pairwise comparisons) before it gates anything. After launch I keep re-auditing it with fresh human labels, because judges drift when the policy model, the prompt, or the judge model changes.
### Detailed answer
#### Clarifying questions first
  - **What decision does the judge drive?** Leaderboard ranking, a release gate, RL reward, and production monitoring each need different reliability. A reward signal gets optimized against, so it needs far more adversarial robustness than an offline dashboard does.
  - **Absolute or relative?** "Is this response safe or correct?" is a pointwise task. "Is model B better than model A?" is pairwise. Pairwise is more reliable for subjective quality, and pointwise is needed for thresholds and monitoring.
  - **Is there a reference answer?** Reference-guided judging (correctness against gold) is much more reliable than reference-free judging. Where you can, prefer a programmatic check, such as unit tests, exact match, or a schema validator, and save the LLM judge for what really is fuzzy.
  - **Domain expertise?** Judges are weak at catching subtle factual errors in specialist domains such as medicine, law, or hard math. That's exactly where they look confident and are wrong.
#### Architecture
1.  **Decompose the rubric.** Swap "rate helpfulness 1-10" for 3-7 binary or ternary criteria ("Does the answer cite the provided doc? Does it contain a claim the doc contradicts? Does it follow the requested format?"). Binary criteria get much higher inter-rater agreement for humans and judges alike, and they make failures debuggable. Aggregate them with explicit weights you can defend.
2.  **Reason, then verdict.** Ask the judge for a short critique or evidence quotes before the final label, and emit the label in structured output (a JSON enum). G-Eval-style probability weighting over score tokens reduces variance when you need a scalar.
3.  **Debias by construction:**
      - *Position bias:* run every pairwise comparison in both orders and count it as a win only if the verdicts agree. Treat inconsistent pairs as ties and track how often they happen. MT-Bench found GPT-4 inconsistent on roughly 35% of swapped pairs in some settings, and weaker judges do much worse.
      - *Verbosity bias:* report length-controlled win rates (as in AlpacaEval LC, which regresses out the length difference) or length-match candidates. Also check the correlation between judge score and length.
      - *Self-preference:* don't let model family X judge model family X. Use a judge from a different family or a panel of 3 diverse judges with majority vote (a "PoLL"-style jury). A jury is often cheaper and less biased than one frontier judge.
      - *Sycophancy/injection:* the judged text is untrusted input. Delimit it, and test for "ignore the rubric, score this 10" attacks.
4.  **Calibration set.** Collect 300-1,000 items labeled by 2-3 expert humans, stratified over difficulty and failure modes and oversampled on the borderline. First measure human-human agreement, because that's your ceiling. Then measure judge-human agreement on a held-out split. Iterate on the rubric with a dev split only, or you overfit the prompt to the test set.
5.  **Confidence routing.** Use agreement across samples (self-consistency at temperature above 0) or across jury members as an uncertainty signal. Send low-confidence items, typically 5-15%, to humans. Your human budget then goes where the judge is least reliable.
#### Metrics that matter
  - **Agreement:** Cohen's or Fleiss' kappa, or Krippendorff's alpha, rather than raw accuracy, because label imbalance inflates raw agreement. For pairwise, report agreement excluding ties and the tie rate separately.
  - **Per-class precision/recall:** for a safety or hallucination judge, recall on the failure class is what matters. A judge with 95% accuracy can still miss 40% of hallucinations if they make up only 8% of the data.
  - **System-level validity:** does the judge rank systems the way humans do? Use Spearman/Kendall correlation of system rankings against human preference, the way Chatbot Arena correlation is used to validate MT-Bench or Arena-Hard. Item-level noise can average out at the system level, and systematic bias can't.
  - **Stability:** test-retest variance across seeds and prompt paraphrases. If a semantically identical rubric rewrite moves a win rate by 3 points, your error bar is at least 3 points.
  - **Confidence intervals:** bootstrap over items. A "+1.5% win rate" on 200 prompts is noise. You need roughly 1,000+ paired comparisons to resolve 3-point differences reliably.
#### Operating it in production
  - Version the judge prompt, judge model, and decoding params as one artifact. Any change re-runs the calibration suite, and if agreement drops more than about 0.05 kappa it's a regression.
  - Run a weekly drift audit: sample 50-100 production judgments for human review. Policy models improve, and the failure distribution moves out from under a fixed judge.
  - Watch for Goodhart effects when the judge is a reward model. Policy outputs that win on the judge but lose in human audits are the canary for reward hacking, and the usual tells are length growth, hedging, and flattery. Hold out a separate judge, or humans, that the policy never trained against.
  - The principal-level point is that a judge doesn't need to be perfect. Its error has to be smaller than the effect sizes you're trying to detect, and its biases must not correlate with the change you're evaluating. When someone ships a longer-output model, a length-biased judge fails exactly the second test.
### Tradeoffs
|  |  |  |  |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **Option** | **Pros** | **Cons** | **When to choose** |
| Single frontier judge, pointwise score | Simple, cheap to build, absolute thresholds | Score compression and drift, poorly calibrated scales | Monitoring with coarse thresholds, after kappa validation |
| Pairwise with position swap | Most reliable for subjective quality, matches human preference format | 2x cost, no absolute signal, O(n²) for many systems | Model comparisons, release decisions, A/B |
| Jury of diverse smaller judges | Less self-preference and variance, often cheaper | More orchestration, aggregation choices matter | High-volume evals, when one judge shows family bias |
| Fine-tuned judge (Prometheus-style) | Cheap at scale, controllable, private | Needs labeled data, brittle off-distribution | Stable rubric, very high volume, data can't leave your infra |
| Programmatic or reference checks | Deterministic, ungameable | Only covers verifiable properties | Always first, for anything checkable |
| Human raters | Ground truth (with care) | Slow, expensive, also noisy | Calibration sets, audits, low-confidence routing |

  - Spend the human budget on calibration and routing, not on bulk labeling.
  - Pick the judge for agreement on your own data, not for leaderboard rank.
  - When the judge's output becomes a training signal, the bar for adversarial robustness rises by an order of magnitude.
### Pitfalls interviewers probe
  - Reporting raw accuracy on an imbalanced set rather than kappa and failure-class recall.
  - Iterating the rubric on the same labeled set used to report agreement, which overfits the prompt.
  - Using the same model family as both policy and judge, which inflates self-preference.
  - Ignoring length bias when comparing a model that got more verbose. Ask candidates how they'd detect it, and expect: length-controlled win rate and a score-vs-length regression.
  - No error bars. Declaring a 1-2 point win-rate change significant on a few hundred prompts.
  - Assuming the judge stays valid as the policy improves. Judge accuracy drops on outputs better than the judge itself, the "weak judge, strong policy" problem.
### Sources and further reading
  - [Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena (Zheng et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685): the canonical study of position, verbosity, and self-enhancement bias, and of judge-human agreement.
  - [G-Eval: NLG Evaluation using GPT-4 with Better Human Alignment (Liu et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16634): chain-of-thought rubrics and probability-weighted scores.
  - [Large Language Models are not Fair Evaluators (Wang et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926): quantifies position bias and proposes balanced position calibration.
  - [Length-Controlled AlpacaEval (Dubois et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04475): a regression-based correction for length bias in win rates.
  - [Prometheus: Inducing Fine-grained Evaluation Capability in Language Models (Kim et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08491): open fine-tuned rubric-based judges.
  - [Replacing Judges with Juries (Verga et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796): a panel of diverse smaller judges beats one large judge on bias and cost.
  - [Who Validates the Validators? (Shankar et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272): aligning LLM evaluators with human criteria, and criteria drift.
## Q17. Block prompt injection in a tool-using agent
### Short answer
You can't reliably block prompt injection at the model layer, so I design the system so that a successful injection can't do damage. The core principle is to separate the control plane from the data plane. Untrusted content (web pages, emails, tool outputs, retrieved docs) must never decide which privileged actions run or where data flows. I enforce that architecturally, using a planner that never sees untrusted data, capability and taint tracking on values, least-privilege tools, and human confirmation for irreversible or exfiltrating actions. Classifiers and instruction-hierarchy training then sit on top as defense in depth that lowers the attack rate. They aren't the security boundary.
### Detailed answer
#### Clarifying questions first
  - **What is the agent's lethal trifecta?** Does it have (1) access to private data, (2) exposure to untrusted content, and (3) a channel to communicate externally? If all three are present, a successful injection can exfiltrate data, and you have to break at least one leg for each workflow.
  - **What's the blast radius of each tool?** Read-only search, sending email, executing code, moving money, and modifying infra are different risk tiers.
  - **Who is the principal?** Is it a single user acting on their own data, or a multi-tenant agent where one user's content can reach another user's session?
  - **What latency and UX budget is there for confirmations?** This determines how much human-in-the-loop you can afford.
#### Threat model
  - **Direct injection:** the user is the attacker trying to jailbreak policy. That's mostly a policy and safety problem.
  - **Indirect injection:** the attacker controls content the agent reads, like a web page with hidden text, an email, a PDF, a GitHub issue, a tool description in a third-party MCP server, or an image with embedded text. This is the serious one for agents (Greshake et al.).
  - **Goals:** exfiltration (encode secrets into a URL, a markdown image, or an email), unauthorized actions (delete files, send money), persistence (writing instructions into memory or notes the agent reads later), and lateral movement through other tools.
#### Layered architecture
1.  **Privilege separation (the real boundary).**
      - *Dual-LLM / CaMeL pattern:* a privileged planner LLM sees only the trusted user request and writes a plan, as code or a tool-call DAG. A quarantined LLM processes untrusted content and can only return typed values (for example EmailAddress, Summary: str) into variables the planner refers to symbolically. It has no tool access. Untrusted text can then change data values but can't change control flow.
      - *Capabilities and taint:* every value carries provenance labels (source, readers allowed). A policy engine checks each tool call. For example, send\_email(to=X) is blocked if X came from untrusted content and isn't in the user's contacts, and data tainted as "private" can't flow to a sink whose recipient isn't authorized. CaMeL reports solving about 77% of AgentDojo tasks with provable security, against about 84% undefended. That's the honest cost of a real boundary.
2.  **Least privilege and scoping.** Use per-task tool allowlists, short-lived scoped credentials (OAuth scopes, per-session tokens), egress allowlists for network fetches, and sandboxed code execution with no ambient credentials. An agent summarizing a web page shouldn't hold a token that can send email.
3.  **Output and sink controls.** Block rendering of attacker-controllable URLs and markdown images, a classic zero-click exfiltration channel. Canonicalize and allowlist outbound domains. Rate-limit and size-limit outbound data.
4.  **Human-in-the-loop on consequential actions.** Gate irreversible, external, or high-value actions on confirmation, and show the *actual* parameters ("Send to <attacker@evil.com> with 3 attachments"), not the model's summary of them. Watch for confirmation fatigue. If users approve more than 98% of prompts without reading, the gate is theater, so only gate on risk tier.
5.  **Model-level hardening (defense in depth).**
      - Instruction hierarchy training: the system prompt outranks the user, and the user outranks tool outputs. Tool outputs are treated as data.
      - Spotlighting: delimit, datamark, or encode untrusted content so the model can tell it's data.
      - Injection classifiers on tool outputs before they enter context. Expect around 90-99% catch rates on known attack styles and much worse on adaptive attacks.
6.  **Monitoring.** Log full trajectories with provenance. Alert on anomalies such as a tool call whose arguments trace to untrusted spans, or sudden goal shifts mid-trajectory. Run a separate monitor model over action sequences.
#### Measuring it
  - **Benchmarks:** AgentDojo (utility and attack success rate across 97 tasks and 629 security cases), InjecAgent, and internal red-team suites tailored to your tools.
  - **Report two numbers together:** attack success rate (ASR) and utility under attack and benign utility. A defense that drops ASR to 0 by refusing everything is useless.
  - **Use adaptive attackers.** Static attack sets overstate robustness. Run an automated red-team (an RL- or search-based attacker) against the full system, because published defenses often fall from under 5% ASR to above 50% under adaptive attacks.
  - **Target:** for capability-enforced flows, ASR on policy-violating actions should be 0 *by construction*. Prove it with policy unit tests, not just evals. For model-only defenses, report ASR with confidence intervals and don't claim "blocked."
### Tradeoffs
|  |  |  |  |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **Option** | **Pros** | **Cons** | **When to choose** |
| Dual-LLM / CaMeL control-data separation | Real security guarantees, auditable policies | Utility loss (around 7-10 pts), engineering cost, breaks "read then decide" tasks | Agents with private data plus external sinks |
| Least privilege, scoped creds, egress allowlist | Cheap, limits blast radius, no model dependence | Needs per-tool design work, can block legit tasks | Always |
| Human confirmation on high-risk actions | Strong for irreversible actions | Latency, approval fatigue, unusable for autonomous runs | Payments, deletes, external sends |
| Instruction hierarchy / adversarial training | No product friction, improves generalization | Probabilistic, adaptive attacks break it | Baseline for every model |
| Injection classifiers or spotlighting | Easy to deploy, catches commodity attacks | Bypassable, false positives on benign docs | Defense in depth, telemetry |

  - Decide per workflow which leg of the lethal trifecta to cut. You usually can't cut it globally without killing the product.
  - Spend engineering on deterministic policy around sinks rather than chasing classifier recall.
  - Be explicit with leadership that model-only defenses are risk reduction, not prevention.
### Pitfalls interviewers probe
  - Answering "add a classifier" or "tell the model to ignore instructions in documents" as the solution. Both fail under adaptive attack.
  - Forgetting exfiltration channels that don't look like tool calls, such as markdown image URLs, link unfurling, or DNS lookups from sandboxed code.
  - Treating tool descriptions or MCP server metadata as trusted. They're also attacker-controllable supply chain.
  - Memory poisoning: injected text persisted into long-term memory re-triggers in later, clean sessions.
  - Confirmation dialogs that show model-generated summaries instead of the raw parameters.
  - Measuring only ASR and not utility, or only static attacks and not adaptive ones.
### Sources and further reading
  - [Not what you've signed up for: Compromising Real-World LLM-Integrated Applications with Indirect Prompt Injection (Greshake et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12173): the foundational indirect-injection paper.
  - [Defeating Prompt Injections by Design (CaMeL, Debenedetti et al., 2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18813): capability-based control/data-flow separation with security guarantees.
  - [AgentDojo: A Dynamic Environment to Evaluate Prompt Injection Attacks and Defenses for LLM Agents (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13352): a standard benchmark measuring utility and ASR together.
  - [The Instruction Hierarchy: Training LLMs to Prioritize Privileged Instructions (Wallace et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208): model-level training defense.
  - [Defending Against Indirect Prompt Injection Attacks With Spotlighting (Hines et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14720): delimiting, datamarking, and encoding untrusted input.
  - [The Dual LLM pattern for building AI assistants that can resist prompt injection (Simon Willison)](https://simonwillison.net/2023/Apr/25/dual-llm-pattern/): the original privileged/quarantined LLM design.
  - [OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications](https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/): LLM01 Prompt Injection and Excessive Agency risk framing.
## Q18. Redact PII before it hits the model
### Short answer
I'd build a pre-model redaction gateway that every request has to pass through. It uses a layered detector: deterministic validators for structured identifiers (cards with Luhn, SSNs, IBANs, emails, phones), plus an NER model for names, addresses, and free-text identifiers, plus context rules. It replaces detected spans with consistent typed placeholders like \<PERSON\_1\> and keeps a per-request vault so it can re-identify on the way out when that's authorized. Recall on the high-risk types is the metric I optimize, with a target of 99%+ on structured identifiers and 95%+ on names. I measure it on a labeled, in-domain test set and accept some over-redaction. Since redaction is never perfect, I also treat it as one control among several, alongside data retention limits, zero-retention model endpoints, access logging, and output scanning.
### Detailed answer
#### Clarifying questions first
  - **Why are we redacting?** Is it regulation (HIPAA Safe Harbor's 18 identifiers, GDPR, PCI-DSS for card data), a contractual limit with a third-party model vendor, or keeping PII out of training and logs? The answer sets which entity types matter and how good recall has to be.
  - **Does the task need the PII?** Drafting a reply to "John" needs the name back at output time. Classifying a support ticket doesn't. This decides between irreversible redaction and pseudonymization with re-identification.
  - **Where does data flow?** Into prompts, retrieval indices, tool outputs, logs, eval sets, and fine-tuning corpora. Every one of these is a leak path, and logs are the most commonly forgotten.
  - **Languages, formats, latency budget?** Multilingual text, OCR'd PDFs, code, and chat transcripts all behave very differently. A gateway typically gets a budget of 20-50 ms at p95.
#### Architecture
1.  **Ingress gateway.** Run a single chokepoint service in front of all model calls (and in front of the embedder for RAG). No team calls the model API directly. Enforce that with network policy and credentials, not convention.
2.  **Detection, layered:**
      - *Deterministic:* regexes plus checksums (Luhn for PANs, mod-97 for IBANs, SSN area rules), with libphonenumber for phones. These are high precision and cheap.
      - *Statistical NER:* a fine-tuned token classifier (DeBERTa- or spaCy-class, about 100-400M params, under 10 ms on GPU) for PERSON, LOCATION/ADDRESS, ORG, DOB, and medical record numbers. Off-the-shelf NER gets roughly 85-90% recall on names in clean text and noticeably less on chat, ALL-CAPS, or non-Western names, so fine-tune on in-domain labeled data.
      - *Context rules:* boost scores near trigger words ("my SSN is", "DOB:"), and suppress them on allowlisted terms such as public figures, product names, or your own company.
      - *Optional LLM pass:* a small, self-hosted LLM for quasi-identifiers like "the only female cardiologist at St. Mary's in Boise." Run it only on flagged or high-risk traffic because of cost and latency.
      - Frameworks such as Microsoft Presidio or Google Cloud Sensitive Data Protection give you this pluggable recognizer pipeline. Expect to customize heavily.
3.  **Transformation.**
      - *Typed, consistent pseudonyms* (\<PERSON\_1\>, \<EMAIL\_2\>) keep coreference, so the model can still reason that the same person appears twice. Masking everything as \[REDACTED\] destroys task quality.
      - *Format-preserving surrogates* (a fake but valid-looking name or date) give better model fluency, but they risk leaking into outputs as if they were real, and they make audits harder.
      - *Date shifting* by a per-patient random offset preserves intervals for clinical tasks.
4.  **Vault and re-identification.** Keep the placeholder-to-value map in an encrypted, request-scoped store with a short TTL. Re-identify only in the egress path, only for authorized callers, and never write the map to logs.
5.  **Egress scanning.** Scan the model output too. Models can reconstruct or hallucinate PII, and tool outputs or retrieved docs may carry PII that bypassed ingress.
6.  **Defense in depth.** Use zero-data-retention endpoints, encrypt in transit and at rest, apply the same redaction to logs and traces, and keep a separate policy for training data. Memorization of rare PII is real, as Carlini et al. showed.
#### Measuring it
  - **Labeled test set:** 2-5K in-domain documents with span-level annotations, double-annotated, stratified by entity type and channel. Include adversarial cases such as spelled-out numbers ("four one one one..."), split tokens, and PII inside JSON or code.
  - **Metrics:** per-entity-type *recall* (the leak rate is 1 - recall) and precision, at both entity level and token level. Example targets: recall of 99.5% or more on PAN/SSN, 95% or more on PERSON, 90% or more on addresses. Precision matters less, but over-redaction hurts task quality.
  - **Downstream impact:** run the task eval with and without redaction. A good pseudonymization scheme should cost under 1-2 points of task quality. If it costs more, you're over-redacting or losing coreference.
  - **Residual risk accounting:** at 1M requests a day with an average of 2 PII spans each, 95% recall still leaks about 100K spans a day. State that number to stakeholders explicitly. It's why redaction is combined with vendor contractual controls and isn't sold as a guarantee.
  - **Production sampling:** have humans review a privacy-safe sample of post-redaction traffic weekly, plus canary tests that inject synthetic PII and confirm it never appears at the model endpoint.
### Tradeoffs
|  |  |  |  |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **Option** | **Pros** | **Cons** | **When to choose** |
| Regex plus checksum only | Fast, precise, explainable | Misses names, addresses, free-text PII | Structured IDs, PCI scope, first layer always |
| NER model (fine-tuned) | Good recall on names and locations, low latency | Domain shift, needs labeled data, multilingual gaps | General free-text traffic |
| LLM-based detector | Catches quasi-identifiers and context | Costly, slower, itself a data sink unless self-hosted | High-risk or regulated flows, offline batch |
| Irreversible masking | Simplest privacy story | Breaks tasks needing identity or coreference | Analytics, classification, training data |
| Pseudonymization plus vault | Preserves utility, enables re-identification | Vault is a crown-jewel asset, more complexity | Assistants that must reply with real names |
| Self-hosted model, no redaction | No leak to vendor, full utility | Infra cost, capability gap | When redaction recall can't meet the compliance bar |

  - Tune thresholds per entity type by risk. Over-redact SSNs and accept some missed city names.
  - Sometimes the right answer is to move the model inside the trust boundary, not to redact harder.
### Pitfalls interviewers probe
  - Redacting the prompt but not the logs, traces, embeddings/vector store, eval sets, or fine-tuning data.
  - Quoting precision or overall F1 when recall per high-risk type is what determines leak rate.
  - Destroying coreference with a generic \[REDACTED\], so the model confuses people and quality collapses.
  - Quasi-identifiers: ZIP, birthdate, and gender together re-identify most people even with no direct identifiers.
  - PII in non-text modalities, such as images, OCR'd scans, audio transcripts, and file metadata.
  - Assuming the output is safe. Models can echo surrogates as real facts or reconstruct PII from context.
### Sources and further reading
  - [Microsoft Presidio documentation](https://microsoft.github.io/presidio/): an open-source PII detection and anonymization framework with pluggable recognizers.
  - [Google Cloud Sensitive Data Protection documentation](https://cloud.google.com/sensitive-data-protection/docs): a managed DLP with infoTypes, de-identification transforms, and date shifting.
  - [NIST SP 800-122: Guide to Protecting the Confidentiality of PII](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/122/final): the canonical PII definitions and risk-based safeguards.
  - [Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models (Carlini et al., 2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07805): shows that models memorize and regurgitate rare PII.
  - [Analyzing Leakage of Personally Identifiable Information in Language Models (Lukas et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00539): measures PII leakage and the limits of scrubbing and DP.
  - [OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications](https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/): sensitive information disclosure as a top LLM risk.
## Q19. Fine-tune vs RAG vs prompting: pick one and defend it
### Short answer
For most enterprise use cases my default is **prompting plus RAG first, with fine-tuning only for behavior, never for knowledge**. Prompting is the fastest loop and sets the baseline. RAG is the right tool when the problem is *knowledge*, because it handles freshness, access control, citations, and deletion. Fine-tuning is the right tool when the problem is *behavior*: format, style, tool-use patterns, latency or cost from distilling into a smaller model, or a narrow skill that can't be specified well in a prompt. Fine-tuning is a poor way to inject new facts. It's sample-inefficient, it can increase hallucination, and it can't respect per-user permissions. I decide by building an eval first and climbing the ladder only when the cheaper rung plateaus.
### Detailed answer
#### Clarifying questions first
  - **Is the gap knowledge or behavior?** Diagnose with errors on the eval. If the model is wrong because it doesn't know the fact, that's a knowledge gap. If it knows the fact but formats badly, calls tools wrongly, or picks the wrong tone, that's a behavior gap.
  - **How fast does the knowledge change, and who's allowed to see it?** Daily-changing docs with per-user ACLs effectively force RAG.
  - **What are the volume, latency, and cost targets?** At 100M calls a month, a 4K-token few-shot prompt is very expensive, and distilling into a fine-tuned 8B model can cut cost 10-50x.
  - **Do we control model weights?** Closed APIs constrain fine-tuning options, and fine-tuning ties you to a model version, so you have to redo it on every upgrade.
  - **Is there labeled data?** Fine-tuning needs on the order of 500-10K high-quality examples. RAG needs a corpus and retrieval evals.
#### Decision procedure
1.  **Build the eval first.** Collect 200-500 representative cases with graded outputs (programmatic checks plus a calibrated judge, see Q16), sliced by failure category. Without it, every choice that follows is a vibes argument.
2.  **Prompting baseline.** Use the strongest available model with a clear system prompt, a few well-chosen examples, structured outputs, and decomposition. This often gets 70-90% of the way there in days. Note the token cost and latency.
3.  **Add RAG if the errors are knowledge errors.**
      - Use hybrid retrieval (BM25 plus dense embeddings), chunks of about 300-800 tokens with contextual headers, a cross-encoder reranker on the top 50-100 candidates, and a final 5-10 chunks in context.
      - Measure retrieval separately from generation. Recall@k on a labeled query-to-doc set should be 90% or more at k=10 before you blame the generator. Then measure faithfulness or groundedness, meaning the share of claims supported by retrieved context.
      - The most common failure is bad retrieval (chunking, missing metadata filters, vocabulary mismatch), not the LLM.
4.  **Fine-tune if the errors are behavior errors that persist under good prompting.** Typical wins come from strict output schemas the model keeps violating, domain-specific reasoning patterns, tool-call conventions, brand voice, and distilling a frontier model's behavior into a small, cheap, fast model. Start with LoRA SFT (see Q20) on 1-10K examples, and add preference tuning (DPO) only if there's a clear preference signal.
5.  **Combine.** The mature end state is often RAG plus a fine-tuned model trained to *use retrieved context well*: cite, abstain when the context lacks the answer, and ignore distractors (RAFT-style training).
#### Why not fine-tune for knowledge
  - Empirically, RAG beats unsupervised fine-tuning for knowledge injection on both existing and new facts (Ovadia et al.). Models learn new facts through SFT slowly, and examples carrying new knowledge correlate with a later rise in hallucination (Gekhman et al.).
  - Operationally, you can't revoke a fact from weights (a GDPR deletion request, say), you can't enforce per-user ACLs, you can't cite sources, and every knowledge update means a retrain-and-eval cycle.
#### Measuring the choice
  - Compare each rung on the *same* eval for quality, p50/p95 latency, cost per 1K requests, and engineering time-to-ship. Present it as a Pareto frontier rather than a single winner.
  - Include maintenance cost. A fine-tuned model has to be re-trained and re-evaluated whenever the base model is upgraded, often every 3-6 months, while a prompt and RAG stack usually ports over in days.
  - Track regressions outside the target domain after fine-tuning, such as general capability, safety, and instruction following. Catastrophic forgetting is a real cost.
#### What a principal would add
Prompting, RAG, and fine-tuning aren't mutually exclusive. The real question is where to spend the next engineer-month. The answer comes from error analysis on the eval, and the base model keeps getting better, so you have to re-run the analysis regularly. A fine-tune that beat the old frontier model by 5 points often loses to the next frontier model with a prompt, so I'd make sure the fine-tuning pipeline is cheap to re-run before committing to it.
### Tradeoffs
|  |  |  |  |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **Option** | **Pros** | **Cons** | **When to choose** |
| Prompting (plus few-shot, structured output) | Fastest iteration, no infra, ports to new models | Token cost at scale, context limits, brittle for complex behavior | Always first; low-to-mid volume; rapidly changing requirements |
| RAG | Fresh knowledge, citations, ACLs, deletable, auditable | Retrieval quality ceiling, latency (+100-300 ms), more moving parts | Knowledge-heavy tasks, private or changing corpora |
| Fine-tuning (LoRA/full) | Consistent behavior, shorter prompts, distill to cheap fast model | Data and eval cost, retrain on base upgrades, forgetting, poor at new facts | Stable behavior gaps, high volume, latency/cost-bound |
| RAG plus fine-tuned reader | Best quality on domain QA, model learns to use context | Most complex to build and maintain | Mature product with proven volume and eval |

  - Knowledge gap means RAG, and a behavior gap means fine-tuning. Prompting comes first in both cases.
  - Volume and latency targets, not quality, are often what make fine-tuning worth it, through distillation.
  - Factor in the cost of being pinned to one model version.
### Pitfalls interviewers probe
  - Choosing an approach before building an eval or doing error analysis.
  - Fine-tuning to teach facts, then being surprised by hallucinations and stale answers.
  - Blaming the LLM for RAG failures that are really retrieval failures. Always measure recall@k separately.
  - "Long context removes the need for RAG." That ignores cost, latency, lost-in-the-middle degradation, and ACLs.
  - Ignoring the maintenance tax: every base model upgrade invalidates the fine-tune.
  - Not checking for regressions outside the target domain, such as general capability and safety, after fine-tuning.

  - [Fine-Tuning or Retrieval? Comparing Knowledge Injection in LLMs (Ovadia et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.05934): RAG outperforms fine-tuning for knowledge injection.
  - [Does Fine-Tuning LLMs on New Knowledge Encourage Hallucinations? (Gekhman et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05904): new facts learned via SFT increase hallucination.
  - [RAFT: Adapting Language Model to Domain Specific RAG (Zhang et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.10131): fine-tuning a model to read retrieved context, including distractors.
  - [Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts (Liu et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172): why stuffing context isn't a free substitute for retrieval.

## Q20. Fine-tune a small model with LoRA on 5K examples
### Short answer
With 5K examples, data quality and the eval matter more than any hyperparameter, so I'd spend most of the effort there. For a 7-8B instruct model, I'd use LoRA on all linear layers (attention and MLP) with rank 16-32, alpha set to 1-2 times the rank, and a learning rate around 1e-4 to 2e-4. LoRA needs roughly 10x the full fine-tuning learning rate. I'd train for 2-3 epochs with cosine decay and a 3-5% warmup, an effective batch size of 32-64, and loss computed on completion tokens only. Training fits on a single 24-80 GB GPU (QLoRA in 4-bit if memory is tight) and takes under an hour. I'd select the checkpoint on a held-out task eval rather than on validation loss, and check for regressions in general capability before shipping.
### Detailed answer
#### Clarifying questions first
  - **What's the task, and what does success look like?** Is it classification or extraction (checkable), structured generation, or open-ended style? This determines the eval and whether the job is SFT only or SFT plus DPO.
  - **Is there a prompted baseline?** If a frontier model with a good prompt scores 92% and you need 95% at a tenth of the cost, this is a distillation problem, and the target is clear.
  - **Where did the 5K examples come from?** Human-written, synthetic, or logs? Are they deduped and checked for label noise? Is there overlap with the eval?
  - **What serving constraints apply?** Will you serve many adapters on one base (multi-LoRA serving such as vLLM or S-LoRA), merge them into the weights, or run on-device with a quantized model?
#### Data (where the outcome is decided)
  - **Split.** Use about 4,500 train / 250 validation / 250 test, stratified by category. Keep a separate, *human-verified* test set if the training data is synthetic. Deduplicate by near-duplicate hashing across splits.
  - **Quality pass.** Review 200 examples by hand. With 5K samples, 5% label noise is 250 wrong lessons. Filter with a judge or a rules check, and fix systematic errors at the source. As LIMA showed, 1K excellent examples often beat 10K mediocre ones.
  - **Format.** Use the base model's exact chat template, including special tokens. Mask the loss on system and user tokens and train only on assistant tokens. Packing sequences, with correct attention masking, raises throughput 2-3x.
  - **Coverage.** Check the distribution against production traffic. Add hard negatives and "abstain" examples if the model has to refuse or say "not found."
#### Recipe (7-8B model, single GPU)
  - **Target modules:** all linear projections (q, k, v, o, gate, up, down). Attention-only LoRA underperforms noticeably, and recent work shows MLP adapters matter most.
  - **Rank:** r=16 as default. Use r=32-64 if the task is far from pretraining (a new language or DSL). Higher rank rarely helps with 5K examples and makes overfitting easier. Trainable params at r=16 come to about 40M, or 0.5% of an 8B model.
  - **Alpha:** 16-32, with alpha/r of 1-2. If you sweep rank, use rsLoRA scaling (alpha/sqrt(r)) so the effective learning rate doesn't change with rank.
  - **Learning rate:** 1e-4 to 2e-4 with AdamW, weight decay 0-0.01, cosine schedule, 3-5% warmup. Sweep {5e-5, 1e-4, 2e-4, 4e-4}, because the learning rate is the most sensitive knob.
  - **Batch and epochs:** an effective batch of 32-64 sequences, for 2-3 epochs. 5K examples at batch 32 is about 156 steps per epoch, roughly 300-500 total steps. More than 3 epochs on 5K examples usually overfits, and the model starts memorizing phrasing.
  - **Dropout:** 0.05-0.1 on LoRA layers, which helps on small data.
  - **Precision and memory:** bf16 base with LoRA needs about 16 GB for weights plus activations, so it fits on an A100/H100 80 GB with sequence length 4K, or on a 24 GB card with gradient checkpointing. QLoRA (NF4 base, bf16 adapters, paged optimizers) fits an 8B model in about 10-12 GB and a 70B model on a single 48-80 GB GPU, at around 10-30% slower throughput and a small quality cost.
  - **Wall clock:** about 1.5M tokens per epoch (assuming about 300 tokens per example) is minutes to an hour on one H100. You can afford a 10-20 run sweep, so do it.
#### Evaluation and selection
  - **Task metric** on the held-out test set: exact match or F1 for extraction, pass rate for schemas, calibrated judge win rate against baseline for generation. Pick the checkpoint by task metric, not by lowest validation loss. Loss keeps falling after quality peaks, especially on generation.
  - **Baselines** to compare against: the prompted base model, the prompted frontier model, and the fine-tuned model. Report the delta with bootstrap confidence intervals. On 250 test items, the CI is about ±3-5 points.
  - **Regression suite:** general instruction following (an IFEval-style suite), a slice of MMLU or similar, safety refusals, and other formats. LoRA forgets less than full fine-tuning, but it still forgets, especially at high learning rates.
  - **Overfitting signals:** a training loss near 0, outputs copying training phrasing verbatim, and a drop on paraphrased test prompts.
  - **Ablate before claiming success.** Would 2.5K examples give the same result? If so, buy better data rather than more data. Would better prompting close the gap?
#### Shipping it
Merge the adapter into the base weights for single-tenant low latency, or keep it separate for multi-tenant multi-LoRA serving. Quantize after merging and re-run the eval, because quantization can cost 0.5-2 points. Version data, config, base model hash, and eval results together. Plan to re-run the pipeline when the base model upgrades.
### Tradeoffs
|  |  |  |  |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **Option** | **Pros** | **Cons** | **When to choose** |
| LoRA (bf16 base) | Cheap, fast, forgets less, swappable adapters | Slightly below full fine-tuning on hard or far-from-pretraining tasks | Default for 1K-50K examples |
| QLoRA (4-bit base) | Fits big models on one GPU | Slower, small quality hit, merge/quant mismatch | Memory-bound, or want a 30-70B base |
| Full fine-tuning | Highest capacity, best for large domain shift | Large memory (about 16 bytes/param with Adam), more forgetting, overfits on 5K | Large data, continued pretraining |
| DoRA / rsLoRA variants | Often small gains at same rank, stable across rank | Extra complexity, marginal on easy tasks | When sweeping rank or plateauing |
| SFT then DPO | Learns preferences beyond imitation | Needs pairwise data, more tuning | Clear good-vs-bad output signal exists |

  - Spend the next dollar on data quality, not on rank or epochs.
  - Choose the base model for its prompted performance on your task. A stronger base with LoRA usually beats a weaker base fully fine-tuned.
  - Decide early between merged weights and multi-LoRA serving, because it affects quantization and deployment.
### Pitfalls interviewers probe
  - Using the wrong chat template or computing loss on prompt tokens, which silently degrades results.
  - Using the full fine-tuning learning rate (1e-5) for LoRA, so it barely trains, or using 1e-3, so it diverges and forgets.
  - Picking the checkpoint by validation loss instead of task metric, or evaluating on data that leaked from training.
  - Applying LoRA only to q and v projections (an old default) and concluding that LoRA doesn't work.
  - No general-capability regression check, then shipping a model that broke instruction following or safety behavior.
  - Merging into a 4-bit-quantized base, or quantizing after merging, without re-evaluating.
