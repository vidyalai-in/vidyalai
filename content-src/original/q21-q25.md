## Q21. Serve an open model at scale (batching, KV cache, quantization)
### Short answer
LLM serving splits into two phases that behave very differently. Prefill is compute-bound. Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound and limited by KV cache capacity. So the design is about keeping the GPUs' HBM bandwidth busy without breaking latency SLOs. I'd use a continuous-batching engine with paged KV cache (vLLM, SGLang or TensorRT-LLM), add chunked prefill and prefix caching, and quantize weights to FP8 or INT4 and the KV cache to FP8 to fit more concurrent sequences per GPU. For parallelism, I'd use tensor parallelism inside an NVLink node and data-parallel replicas across nodes. I'd size the fleet from tokens/sec per replica at the TTFT and TPOT SLO, not from raw peak throughput, and I'd judge success by goodput: requests per second that actually meet the SLO, per dollar.
### Detailed answer
#### Clarifying questions first
  - **Model and architecture.** Is it dense (Llama-3.1-70B) or MoE (DeepSeek-V3, Mixtral)? What is the GQA ratio? MoE changes the parallelism plan (expert parallelism) and the memory math.
  - **Traffic shape.** Peak QPS, input/output token distributions, and how much prompt prefix is shared (system prompts, RAG, multi-turn chat).
  - **SLOs.** TTFT p50/p99 (for example under 500 ms / under 2 s), TPOT or inter-token latency (for example under 50 ms, which is at least 20 tok/s per user, faster than people read), and availability.
  - **Interactive vs batch.** Offline jobs can run at huge batch sizes with no latency constraint. Mixing them with interactive traffic needs priority scheduling.
  - **Quality tolerance.** Does quantization need to stay within, say, 0.5 pt on the eval suite, and which evals count?
#### Where the bottleneck sits (Llama-3.1-70B, H100 80GB)
  - **Weights.** 70B params: about 140 GB in BF16, about 70 GB in FP8, about 38 GB in INT4 (AWQ/GPTQ with group scales).
  - **KV cache per token.** 2 (K and V) x 80 layers x 8 KV heads x 128 head\_dim x 2 bytes = **\~320 KB/token in BF16**, or \~160 KB in FP8. Without GQA (64 KV heads) it would be 2.5 MB/token. This is why GQA and MLA matter for serving.
  - **Capacity.** Take TP=4 on 4xH100 (320 GB) with FP8 weights and about 90% memory utilisation. That leaves roughly 288 - 70 - activations/workspace (\~15 GB), so about 200 GB for KV, which is about 1.25M tokens in FP8. At 4K average context that supports **about 300 concurrent sequences**.
  - **Decode step time.** Each step reads all weights plus the active KV. With 17.5 GB of weights per GPU at 3.35 TB/s, the floor is about 5 ms/step. Add KV reads (128 seqs x 4K x 160 KB ≈ 84 GB spread over 4 GPUs ≈ 6 ms) plus communication and kernel overhead, and a realistic figure is about 20-30 ms/step. At batch 128, that is **about 4-6k output tok/s per replica** at roughly 35-50 tok/s per user.
  - **Prefill.** About 2 x 70B = 140 GFLOP/token. An H100 at FP8 sustains about 800 TFLOPS at around 40% MFU, so a 4-GPU replica does about 20-25k prefill tok/s. A 4K-token prompt therefore costs about 170 ms of compute, before any queueing.
#### Batching
  - **Static batching** wastes slots: the whole batch waits for its longest sequence. **Continuous (iteration-level) batching** (Orca) admits and evicts sequences at every decode step. It gives 3-10x more throughput than static batching.
  - **Chunked prefill** (Sarathi-Serve) splits long prompts into chunks of about 512-2048 tokens and mixes them into decode steps. Without it, one 32K-token prompt stalls every decoding user for about 1.5 s, which shows up as p99 TPOT spikes.
  - **Scheduler policy.** Set a max-tokens-per-step budget, use priority classes (interactive over batch), and preempt by recompute or swap when KV runs out. Admission control rejects or queues new requests early instead of thrashing.
#### KV cache management
  - **PagedAttention.** Fixed-size blocks (16 tokens) with block tables. Fragmentation drops from 60-80% waste to under 4%, and copy-on-write supports beam search and parallel sampling.
  - **Prefix caching** (vLLM APC, SGLang RadixAttention) reuses KV for shared system prompts and multi-turn history. With a 2K-token system prompt on every request, this can cut prefill compute by 50%+. It only pays off if the router is **cache-aware**, sending requests that share a prefix to the same replica (consistent hashing on a prefix hash, with load-balancing slack).
  - **KV quantization** to FP8 doubles concurrency at a small quality cost. Validate it on long-context evals, because errors accumulate.
  - **Offload tiers.** HBM, then host DRAM, then SSD for idle multi-turn sessions (the Mooncake-style design). Restoring a session's KV over PCIe is far cheaper than recomputing prefill.
#### Quantization
  - **Weight-only INT4** (AWQ, GPTQ) mainly helps decode, which is bandwidth-bound: about 2-3x faster at small batch. Gains shrink at large batch, where the work becomes compute-bound and dequantization overhead appears.
  - **FP8 W8A8** on Hopper and Blackwell speeds up both prefill and decode and is usually within noise on quality for 70B+ models. It is my default for production.
  - **Validation gate.** Run the full eval suite (not only perplexity): long-context retrieval, code, tool-call JSON validity and multilingual. Small models (7-8B) and low-resource languages degrade first.
#### Parallelism and topology
  - Keep **TP inside an NVLink domain** (TP=4 or 8). Use **PP or replicas across nodes**. For large MoE models, add **expert parallelism** with all-to-all communication.
  - At scale, consider **prefill/decode disaggregation** (DistServe, Splitwise): separate pools, with KV shipped over RDMA. This removes prefill-decode interference and lets each pool be sized independently. It is worth the complexity once p99 TPOT is dominated by prefill interference or the input:output ratio is skewed.
  - **Speculative decoding** (draft model, Medusa or EAGLE heads) gives 1.5-3x lower latency at low batch. The gain disappears at high batch, where decode is no longer bandwidth-starved, so enable it adaptively based on load.
#### Fleet sizing example
Assume a peak of 1,000 req/s, 1,500 input and 300 output tokens per request:
  - Decode: 300k tok/s divided by about 5k per replica gives about 60 replicas.
  - Prefill: 1.5M tok/s with 50% prefix-cache hits leaves 750k tok/s, divided by about 22k per replica, which is about 35 replicas' worth of compute. This mixes in through chunked prefill, so the total is about 80-90 replicas.
  - Add 30% headroom for failures, deploys and diurnal error: about 110 replicas x 4 GPUs, or **\~450 H100s**.
  - At about $2.50/GPU-hr that is roughly $27k/day, or about $0.03 per 1K output tokens all-in.
#### How to measure
  - **Goodput** at the SLO (requests/s meeting both TTFT and TPOT).
  - TTFT and TPOT distributions split by input-length bucket.
  - KV utilisation, preemption rate, prefix-cache hit rate, MFU and memory-bandwidth utilisation, and cost per million tokens.
  - Load-test with a replay of production traces, not uniform synthetic prompts.
### Tradeoffs
|  |  |  |  |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **Option** | **Pros** | **Cons** | **When to choose** |
| BF16, TP=8 | Max quality, simple | 2x memory and cost vs FP8; less KV room | Quality-critical evals, small fleets |
| FP8 W8A8 + FP8 KV | \~2x capacity, faster prefill and decode, near-lossless at 70B+ | Needs Hopper+; calibration; long-context drift | Default for production on H100/B200 |
| INT4 weight-only (AWQ/GPTQ) | Fits 70B on 1-2 GPUs; fast low-batch decode | Quality loss on small models; weaker at high batch | Cost-sensitive, latency-bound, smaller deployments |
| Colocated prefill+decode with chunked prefill | One pool, simpler ops, good utilisation | Residual interference in p99 TPOT | Most deployments up to \~hundreds of GPUs |
| Disaggregated prefill/decode | Independent scaling; best p99 TPOT | KV transfer (RDMA) complexity; more failure modes | Very large fleets, long prompts, strict TPOT |
| Speculative decoding | 1.5-3x lower latency at low load | Wasted compute at high batch; draft maintenance | Latency-sensitive, low-to-medium concurrency |

  - **Throughput vs latency is a single dial (max batch size).** Choose it from the SLO and maximise goodput under that constraint. Never quote peak tok/s without the latency attached.
  - **Cache-aware routing vs even load balancing.** Prefix-hit locality fights load spreading. Use bounded-load consistent hashing.
  - **Build vs buy the engine.** Use vLLM or SGLang and contribute upstream. Custom kernels only pay off for the top two or three hot paths.
### Pitfalls interviewers probe
  - Ignoring that **decode is memory-bound**: saying "add more FLOPs" or treating GPU utilisation % as a capacity metric (nvidia-smi utilisation means nothing here).
  - Getting the **KV cache math** wrong, or forgetting that it grows with context x concurrency and that long-context users can starve everyone else. Per-tenant token budgets are needed.
  - Treating **quantization as free**: no eval gate, only perplexity checked, no checks on tool-calling, JSON validity or long context.
  - Missing **head-of-line blocking from long prefills** and **preemption storms** when KV runs out, both of which show up as p99 collapse under load.
  - Load-testing with fixed-length synthetic prompts, which hides real tails and prefix-sharing effects.
  - Forgetting **cold start**: loading 70-140 GB of weights takes minutes. Plan for pre-warmed pools, safetensors streaming, and autoscaling on queue depth and KV utilisation rather than CPU.
### Sources and further reading
  - [Efficient Memory Management for LLM Serving with PagedAttention (vLLM)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180): paged KV cache, the basis of modern engines.
  - [Orca: A Distributed Serving System for Transformer-Based Generative Models (OSDI '22)](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi22/presentation/yu): iteration-level (continuous) batching.
  - [Taming Throughput-Latency Tradeoff in LLM Inference with Sarathi-Serve](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02310): chunked prefill and stall-free scheduling.
  - [DistServe: Disaggregating Prefill and Decoding for Goodput-optimized LLM Serving](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09670): disaggregation and the goodput metric.
  - [AWQ: Activation-aware Weight Quantization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00978) and [GPTQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.17323): the two standard INT4 weight-only methods.
  - [Fast Inference from Transformers via Speculative Decoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192): the original speculative decoding paper.
  - [SGLang / RadixAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07104): prefix-sharing KV cache via a radix tree.
  - [Efficiently Scaling Transformer Inference (Pope et al.)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05102): partitioning and latency/MFU tradeoffs for inference at scale.

## Q22. Stream responses over SSE and handle disconnects
### Short answer
Server-sent events are a simple fit for token streaming. They are one-way and plain HTTP, and they work through most proxies. You send text/event-stream with id:, event: and data: fields, flush small token batches every \~20-50 ms, and send heartbeat comments to keep intermediaries from timing out. The hard part is disconnects. Generation should be decoupled from the HTTP connection: the model writes tokens to a short-lived per-message log keyed by message ID, and the SSE handler tails that log. A dropped client can then resume with Last-Event-ID. Whether generation continues or gets cancelled after a disconnect is an explicit product and cost policy. When it is cancelled, the cancellation must reach the inference engine so the KV cache and GPU slot are freed.
### Detailed answer
#### Clarifying questions
  - **Client type.** Browser, mobile app or API SDK? Browsers' EventSource only supports GET with no custom headers, so for POST bodies and auth headers, use fetch() with a ReadableStream parser.
  - **On disconnect.** Keep generating (a consumer chat app where the user switched tabs) or abort (an API where the client is gone and cost matters)?
  - **Resumability.** Must a refresh or network blip resume mid-answer, or is "show the final answer when it's done" enough?
  - **Infrastructure.** Which CDN, load balancer and proxy layers sit in front, and what are their idle timeouts and buffering behaviour?
#### Wire format and server mechanics
  - Headers: Content-Type: text/event-stream, Cache-Control: no-cache, X-Accel-Buffering: no (nginx), and no gzip on the stream (or flush-aware compression), because buffering kills the streaming feel.
  - Frames: id: \<msg\_id\>:\<seq\>\\n event: delta\\n data: {"t":"Hello"}\\n\\n. Use typed events: delta, tool\_call, usage, error and done. A final done event (or \[DONE\]) lets the client tell **completion** apart from **truncation**.
  - **Coalescing.** A 70B model at \~40 tok/s emits a token every 25 ms. Flush every 20-50 ms or every N tokens. Per-token writes at 10k concurrent streams cost syscalls and TLS records for no visible benefit.
  - **Heartbeats.** Send : ping\\n\\n every 15 s. AWS ALB's default idle timeout is 60 s, as is nginx's proxy\_read\_timeout, and "thinking" models or slow tool calls can go silent for longer than that.
  - **Errors mid-stream.** HTTP 200 has already been sent, so errors must be in-band (event: error with a code and a retryable flag). Clients must treat a stream that closes without done as an error.
  - **HTTP/2 or 3 end to end.** Under HTTP/1.1, browsers cap you at about 6 connections per origin, and a user with several tabs can deadlock.
#### Architecture for disconnect resilience
1.  POST /messages validates the request, writes a message row (status generating) and an idempotency key, enqueues the generation, and returns a message\_id (or upgrades to the stream directly).
2.  The **generation worker** calls the inference engine and appends tokens or chunks to a **per-message stream log**: Redis Streams or an in-memory ring buffer with a TTL of about 10 min, keyed by message\_id, with monotonically increasing sequence numbers.
3.  The **SSE gateway** (GET /messages/{id}/stream) reads from Last-Event-ID (or from 0) and tails the log. Gateways are stateless, so any replica can resume any stream.
4.  On completion, the worker persists the final text to the durable message store, marks the message complete, writes a done event, and the log expires.
5.  **Client.** On a network error, reconnect with exponential backoff plus jitter and Last-Event-ID. Deduplicate by sequence number and render idempotently. If the log has expired, fetch the completed message from the REST API.
#### Disconnect detection and cancellation
  - Detect through the request context (ctx.Done() in Go, request.is\_disconnected() in Starlette) or a write error. TCP can take a long time to notice a dead peer, so heartbeat writes double as liveness probes.
  - **Policy matrix.**
      - Consumer chat: keep generating, up to a cap, so the answer is there when the user returns.
      - API: abort after a short grace period (\~2-5 s) to allow reconnects.
      - Explicit user "Stop": abort immediately, persist the partial answer marked stopped.
  - **Propagate the cancel all the way down.** Gateway, then worker, then engine abort(request\_id), which frees KV blocks and the batch slot. A dropped cancel means a 32K-context zombie sequence holds about 10 GB of KV (on 70B BF16) and keeps generating to max\_tokens. At scale, zombie generations can waste 5-15% of decode capacity. Track a "tokens generated after client gone" metric.
  - **Billing.** Bill tokens actually generated up to the cancel, and record it in the usage event.
  - **Tool calls in agents.** Don't abort halfway through a side-effecting tool call. Mark cancel points between steps.
#### Deploys and scale
  - Long-lived streams make deploys hard. Use **connection draining** with a max stream duration, and send a reconnect event so the client resumes on a new pod. That is safe because state lives in the log, not the pod.
  - **Capacity math.** At 700 new streams/s with about 12 s mean duration, there are roughly 8,400 concurrent streams. One async gateway pod handles 10-50k idle-ish connections, so the gateway is cheap. The limits are file descriptors, LB connection limits and Redis fan-out (about 700 x 40 tok/s ≈ 28k appends/s, coalesced to \~3-5k writes/s).
#### How to measure
  - Client-measured TTFT (first visible token) and inter-chunk gaps at p99.
  - Percentage of streams ending without done, and the resume success rate.
  - Abort-propagation latency (client gone until KV freed) and zombie token rate.
  - Gateway connection count and heartbeat-timeout rate, split by proxy path.
### Tradeoffs
|  |  |  |  |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **Option** | **Pros** | **Cons** | **When to choose** |
| SSE (fetch + stream parser) | Plain HTTP, proxy- and CDN-friendly, built-in resume semantics, easy to debug | One-way; HTTP/1.1 connection limits; proxies can buffer | Default for LLM token streaming |
| WebSockets | Bidirectional, low overhead per message | Sticky, stateful; LB and proxy quirks; you build your own resume | Realtime voice, collaborative editing, interactive tool approval mid-stream |
| Long-polling / chunked JSON | Works everywhere | Higher latency, more requests | Legacy clients, very restrictive networks |
| Couple generation to the connection | Simplest; auto-cancel saves GPU | Any blip loses the answer; no resume | Prototypes, internal tools |
| Decoupled generation + stream log | Resumable, deploy-safe, multi-device | Redis/log infra; must enforce cancel explicitly | Production consumer chat |

  - **Keep vs cancel on disconnect** is a product, cost and safety decision. Make it per surface and measurable.
  - **Flush cadence.** Smoothness and perceived speed against syscall and network overhead. 20-50 ms works for most.
### Pitfalls interviewers probe
  - **Proxy buffering and idle timeouts.** Nginx, CDN or corporate proxies buffer the whole response or kill it at 60 s. The fixes are heartbeats, disabling buffering, and testing through the real edge.
  - **Not propagating cancellation to the engine**, which leaves zombie generations that hold KV cache and capacity.
  - **Treating TCP close as completion.** There is no terminal done event, so truncated answers get persisted as if they were complete.
  - **Reconnect storms after a gateway deploy or outage** with no jitter or backoff, causing a thundering herd on the stream log and auth service.
  - **Non-idempotent retries.** A client retries the POST and triggers two generations (double cost, duplicate messages). Use idempotency keys.
  - **Auth with EventSource**, which cannot set headers. Putting tokens in query strings leaks them into logs.
### Sources and further reading
  - [MDN: Using server-sent events](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Server-sent_events/Using_server-sent_events): event format, id, retry, reconnection.
  - [WHATWG HTML Standard: Server-sent events](https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/server-sent-events.html): the normative spec, including Last-Event-ID semantics.
  - [MDN: AbortController](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/AbortController): client-side cancellation of fetch streams.
  - [Anthropic API: Streaming Messages](https://docs.anthropic.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/streaming): a production typed-event SSE schema (message\_start, content\_block\_delta, message\_stop, error).
  - [OpenAI API reference: Streaming](https://platform.openai.com/docs/api-reference/streaming): another widely used SSE contract for LLM tokens.
  - [Efficient Memory Management for LLM Serving with PagedAttention (vLLM)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180): why freeing KV on abort matters for capacity.

## Q23. Trace every step of a multi-agent workflow
### Short answer
I'd make each user task a single distributed trace using OpenTelemetry and its GenAI semantic conventions. Each agent invocation, LLM call, tool execution, retrieval and handoff becomes a span, joined by W3C trace context that is propagated explicitly across queues and async boundaries. Spans carry structured metadata: model, prompt version, token usage, cost, latency, finish reason and tool status. Prompt and completion content goes to a separate access-controlled blob store, referenced from the span. I'd use tail-based sampling so every error, expensive run and flagged run is kept and normal traffic is sampled. The goal isn't pretty waterfalls. It is answering three questions quickly: why did this run fail, what changed, and where do the cost and latency go.
### Detailed answer
#### Clarifying questions
  - **Topology.** Orchestrator-worker, peer handoffs, or a graph (LangGraph-style)? Synchronous calls or queue-based fan-out? Do runs last seconds or hours?
  - **Scale.** Runs per day and steps per run, which drive storage and sampling.
  - **Data sensitivity.** Is PII or customer data in prompts? What retention and residency rules apply? Who may view raw content?
  - **Consumers.** On-call debugging, eval and quality teams, finance (cost attribution), or safety (audit trails)? Each needs different retention and fidelity.
#### Span model
  - **Root span:** workflow.run (task\_id, user/tenant hash, entrypoint, workflow version).
  - **Agent spans:** invoke\_agent \<name\> (agent id and role, gen\_ai.agent.name, prompt template version, max-steps budget).
  - **LLM spans:** chat \<model\>, using GenAI semconv attributes: gen\_ai.operation.name, gen\_ai.request.model, gen\_ai.response.model, gen\_ai.usage.input\_tokens and output\_tokens, cached tokens, gen\_ai.response.finish\_reasons, temperature, plus TTFT and the derived cost.
  - **Tool spans:** execute\_tool \<name\> (tool version, arguments hash, status, retry count, side-effecting flag, downstream HTTP spans as children).
  - **Retrieval spans:** query, index version, top-k doc IDs and scores.
  - **Handoffs and fan-in.** Parent-child for delegation. **Span links** when an aggregator consumes outputs from N parallel workers, or when a resumed run continues an earlier trace.
  - **Events on spans:** guardrail verdicts, planner decisions ("chose tool X"), loop-detection triggers, human-approval waits.
#### Context propagation (where most implementations break)
  - In-process: OTel context with contextvars. Thread pools and asyncio task groups often **drop context** unless they are wrapped.
  - Across services: traceparent and tracestate in HTTP or gRPC headers. **Across queues:** inject into message headers (Kafka, SQS, Pub/Sub) and extract in the consumer.
  - Long-running or human-in-the-loop runs: persist the trace context in the workflow state store (Temporal or another durable executor) so a run resumed hours later stays attached. Use links if the trace would otherwise grow unbounded.
  - Put trace\_id into every log line and into the API response (x-request-id) so support tickets map to traces.
#### Content capture and privacy
  - The span attributes stay small and structured. **Full prompts and completions** go to an object store, encrypted, keyed by span id, with a pointer attribute. This keeps the tracing backend cheap and lets content get stricter ACLs and shorter retention (for example 30 days for content, 13 months for metadata).
  - Redact or tokenize PII at the SDK before export. Never log secrets from tool arguments, using allowlisted argument fields.
  - Record **enough to replay**: exact rendered prompt, model snapshot, sampling params and seed, tool results. A failing run can then be re-executed deterministically against a new prompt or model, which turns traces into eval cases.
#### Volume math and sampling
  - Example: 2M runs/day x about 40 spans x about 1.5 KB metadata ≈ **120 GB/day** of span data. Content averages about 15 KB per LLM span x 15 LLM spans per run, roughly **450 GB/day**.
  - **Head sampling** (decide at the root) is cheap but throws away the rare failures you most need.
  - **Tail sampling** in the OTel Collector (load-balanced by trace\_id) decides after the trace completes:
      - keep 100% of errors, guardrail trips, runs over p95 cost or latency, and runs with negative user feedback;
      - keep 100% for new prompt or model versions during rollout;
      - keep 1-5% of the rest.
  - **Separately, emit unsampled metrics** (RED metrics, tokens, cost per agent, steps per run) from spans via a span-metrics connector. Aggregates then stay accurate whatever the sampling rate. Keep high-cardinality fields (user\_id, prompt text) out of metric labels.
#### Making traces useful
  - **Derived signals:** cost per successful task, steps-to-completion distribution, tool error rate by tool version, re-plan and loop rate (the same tool with the same args repeated more than 3 times), agent-to-agent token amplification (a sub-agent fan-out multiplying tokens 10-15x is common).
  - **Online evals attached to traces.** LLM-judge or heuristic scores and user feedback written back as span attributes or linked records. You can then filter for "runs with judge score \< 3 after prompt v42".
  - **Diffing.** Compare trace shape distributions before and after a deploy, for example average tool calls per run jumping from 6 to 11.
  - **Version stamps on every span** (prompt, model, tool and workflow versions) make "what changed?" a query instead of an investigation.
#### How to measure success
  - Mean time to root-cause for agent incidents (for example from hours to under 15 min).
  - Percentage of production failures convertible to replayable eval cases.
  - Trace completeness: share of traces with no orphan spans or broken context. Target \>99%, enforced by a CI test that runs a sample workflow and asserts the span tree.
  - Tracing overhead under 1-2% of latency and a known cost per million spans.
### Tradeoffs
|  |  |  |  |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **Option** | **Pros** | **Cons** | **When to choose** |
| OTel + GenAI semconv to a general backend (Tempo, Honeycomb, Datadog) | Vendor-neutral; joins LLM spans with infra spans; one pipeline | GenAI UX (prompt diffing, eval views) is thinner | Platform teams; agents embedded in larger systems |
| LLM-specific platforms (Langfuse, LangSmith, Arize Phoenix) | Prompt and completion views, eval and dataset loops built in | Another silo; may miss non-LLM spans; data residency | Fast-moving agent teams; can still ingest OTel |
| Head sampling | Simple, cheap, predictable | Drops rare failures | Very high volume, uniform traffic |
| Tail sampling | Keeps errors and outliers | Collector state, memory, trace-aware LB | Default for agent workloads |
| Inline content in spans | Easy debugging | Cost, PII exposure, attribute size limits | Dev and staging only |
| Content in a separate blob store | Cheap, ACL-able, separate retention | Extra hop; pointers can dangle | Production |

  - **Fidelity vs privacy and cost.** Capture structure for everything, content for a sampled or flagged subset, and replay data for failures.
  - **Instrument the framework, not each agent.** Auto-instrument the LLM client, tool registry and handoff primitive so coverage doesn't depend on developer discipline.
### Pitfalls interviewers probe
  - **Broken context propagation** across async, thread pools and queues, which produces orphan spans and fragmented traces exactly where multi-agent bugs live.
  - **Head-based sampling** that discards the 0.5% of failing runs you needed to see.
  - **Logging raw prompts with PII or secrets** into a broadly accessible observability tool, which becomes a compliance incident.
  - **Cardinality explosions** from putting user\_id, prompt hashes or model outputs into metric labels.
  - **Streaming spans** that end at the first token, so latency and token counts are wrong. A span must close at stream end and record TTFT as an attribute or event.
  - **No version stamps.** Without them you can't correlate a regression to a prompt, model or tool change, and tracing becomes a waterfall picture rather than a debugging tool.
### Sources and further reading
  - [OpenTelemetry Semantic Conventions for Generative AI](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/semconv/gen-ai/): the standard attribute names for LLM operations.
  - [OTel GenAI agent and framework spans](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/semconv/gen-ai/gen-ai-agent-spans/): conventions for invoke\_agent and agent spans.
  - [OpenTelemetry: Sampling concepts](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/concepts/sampling/): head vs tail sampling tradeoffs.
  - [W3C Trace Context](https://www.w3.org/TR/trace-context/): the traceparent and tracestate propagation standard.
  - [Dapper, a Large-Scale Distributed Systems Tracing Infrastructure](https://research.google/pubs/dapper-a-large-scale-distributed-systems-tracing-infrastructure/): Google's foundational tracing paper (sampling, low overhead, annotations).
  - [Anthropic: How we built our multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/built-multi-agent-research-system): production lessons on observability, token amplification and debugging multi-agent systems.
  - [Anthropic: Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents): agent patterns (orchestrator-workers and others) that shape the span topology.

## Q24. Roll back a bad prompt change in production
### Short answer
The rollback itself should be boring: a pointer flip. Prompts are immutable, versioned artifacts in a registry, and production resolves an alias (chat-system@prod -\> v42) through a config service with push invalidation. Rolling back means pointing the alias at the last known-good version, and it reaches every server in under a minute without a code deploy. The real engineering is in three things around it. Detect fast, using a canary with automated guardrail metrics and every trace stamped with the prompt version. Keep prompt and code changes backward-compatible so the old version still works. Clean up the after-effects: conversations, memories and downstream data written while the bad prompt was live, plus a regression eval so the same failure can't ship again.
### Detailed answer
#### Clarifying questions
  - **Blast radius.** One feature or the global system prompt? All tenants or some? Is it a safety, correctness or tone problem? A safety regression means an immediate kill switch, with no time spent on analysis.
  - **Coupling.** Did the prompt change ship with code changes (new template variables, tool schemas, output parsers) or a model snapshot change? Was v41 tested against the current code?
  - **Persisted effects.** Did outputs get stored, acted on (tool side effects, emails sent) or fed into other systems (memory, summaries, fine-tuning data)?
#### Prerequisites (what makes rollback a 2-minute operation)
  - **Prompt registry.** Immutable versions with content hash, author, linked eval results, target model snapshot and required template variables. Aliases (dev, canary, prod) are mutable pointers, and changes are audit-logged.
  - **Serving path.** Apps fetch by alias through a config service (or feature-flag platform) with a local cache and push invalidation (or a TTL under 30 s), plus a **last-known-good fallback** baked into the binary if the registry is down.
  - **Compatibility contract.** Templates declare their inputs, and the renderer validates at load time. The code supports **N and N-1** prompt versions, so a rollback can't crash on a missing variable. Prompt+code changes go in two phases: ship code that supports both, then flip the prompt.
  - **Version stamping.** prompt.id, prompt.version and model.snapshot go on every trace, log and feedback event. Without this you can't attribute a regression.
#### Rollout that catches problems before 100%
1.  **Offline eval gate.** Golden set (for example 1-3k cases) plus targeted regression suites: format validity, tool-call accuracy, refusal and over-refusal, safety red-team, length, latency and cost. Block on regressions beyond agreed thresholds.
2.  **Shadow mode** (optional). Run the new prompt on mirrored traffic with no user exposure and compare with LLM-judge pairwise scoring.
3.  **Canary** at 1%, then 5%, 25%, 50% and 100%, with automated analysis at each stage (Kayenta-style: canary vs a concurrent control, not vs yesterday).
4.  **Guardrail metrics.**
      - Fast, deterministic: parse and schema error rate, tool-call failure rate, refusal rate, empty or truncated responses, output tokens per response (cost), TTFT.
      - Slower, noisy: thumbs-down rate, regenerate rate, conversation abandonment, sampled LLM-judge scores.
5.  **Statistical power.** Detecting a 1 pp rise in thumbs-down on a 5% base needs about 16·p(1-p)/δ² ≈ 7,600 rated responses per arm. If only 2% of responses get rated, that is about 380k responses per arm. **So deterministic metrics and LLM judges are what catch problems in minutes. User feedback confirms over hours.**
#### The rollback itself
  - **Trigger.** An automated rollback when a guardrail breaches during canary. An on-call one-click rollback (or a CLI prompt rollback chat-system --to v41) otherwise. A **kill switch** for safety issues routes to a conservative fallback prompt or disables the feature.
  - **Propagation.** Config push, then caches invalidated, then the new requests in flight use v41. Verify through the dashboard filtered by prompt.version: v42 traffic should drop to zero within \~60 s.
  - **In-flight conversations.** Decide whether multi-turn sessions started on v42 switch mid-conversation (usually yes; the system prompt is re-rendered each turn) or are pinned. For agents mid-task, finish the current step, then pick up v41.
  - **Expect side effects of rolling back.** **Prompt cache invalidation**: the provider or engine prefix cache for v41 may be cold, causing a temporary TTFT and cost spike. Warm it, or tolerate it.
#### After the rollback
  - **Data remediation.** Find affected records by version stamp (conversation summaries, agent memory, extracted fields, tickets). Re-run, flag or notify as appropriate. This is usually harder than the rollback.
  - **Root cause.** Why did evals miss it? Typical answers: the golden set lacked the slice (a language, long conversations, a tenant's tools), the change interacted with a silent model snapshot update, or the judge was miscalibrated.
  - **Close the loop.** Add the failing traces as regression cases, which is easy if traces are replayable (see Q23). Add the missed metric to canary guardrails. Blameless postmortem.
  - **Targets.** Time to detect under 15 min for deterministic regressions. Time to mitigate under 5 min. Zero incidents caused by an incompatible rollback.
### Tradeoffs
|  |  |  |  |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **Option** | **Pros** | **Cons** | **When to choose** |
| Prompts in code, rolled back via redeploy | Code review, atomic with code, simple | Rollback takes 10-60 min of CI/CD; couples to unrelated changes | Small teams, prompts tightly coupled to parsers |
| Prompt registry + alias via config/flag service | Sub-minute rollback; per-tenant targeting; audit trail | Code/prompt skew risk; needs a compatibility contract | Production LLM products at scale |
| Fixed-percentage canary with automated analysis | Catches regressions with small blast radius | Slow for low-signal metrics; needs a control arm | Default for prompt changes |
| Full A/B experiment | Measures real product impact | Days to reach power; exposure to a bad variant | Intentional behaviour changes, not fixes |
| Auto-rollback on guardrail breach | Fast, no human in the loop | False positives from noisy metrics; flapping | Deterministic metrics (parse errors, refusals, cost) |

  - **Treat prompts like code *****and***** like config.** Review, evals and versioning like code. Deployment and rollback speed like config.
  - **Pin model snapshots.** A prompt is tuned against a specific model version. Unpinned "latest" aliases make rollback insufficient because the regression may come from the model.
### Pitfalls interviewers probe
  - **"Just revert the git commit"**: a slow, coupled rollback with no ability to target a subset of traffic.
  - **Rollback that breaks.** The old prompt references variables or tools the new code removed, or the parser expects the new output format.
  - **No version on traces**, so you can't prove the prompt caused it, or tell when the fix took effect.
  - **Relying only on user feedback** for detection. It is too sparse and too slow, as the power math shows.
  - **Ignoring persisted contamination**: memories, summaries or downstream records written under the bad prompt, and any use of those outputs as training data.
  - **Confounding changes.** The model provider updated a snapshot, or a retrieval index changed at the same time, and you rolled back the wrong thing.

## Q25. Design ChatGPT-style chat for 1M daily users
### Short answer
At 1M DAU the traffic is about 20M messages/day, roughly 250 msg/s on average and \~750/s at peak, with \~8-10k concurrent streams. That is modest for the web tier and substantial for GPUs, so the design centres on the inference layer and its cost. The architecture is:
  - a stateless API and an SSE streaming gateway;
  - a conversation service backed by a sharded store (conversations partitioned by user, messages by conversation);
  - an orchestration layer that builds context (history truncation or summarisation, retrieval, tools, safety);
  - a GPU inference fleet with continuous batching, prefix caching and cache-affinity routing.
The principal-level concerns are cost per DAU, p99 TTFT under load spikes, graceful degradation (model fallback, queueing, rate limits), and safety and abuse controls. Those matter more than the CRUD.
### Detailed answer
#### Clarifying questions
  - **Model.** Self-hosted open model or a vendor API? One model or a tiered router (small, fast model for simple queries; a frontier model for hard ones)?
  - **Features.** Multimodal, file uploads, web search, tools, memory, sharing links? Each adds a subsystem.
  - **SLOs and economics.** TTFT p95 \< 1 s, streaming ≥ 30 tok/s, 99.9% availability. What is the cost budget per DAU, and is there a free/paid split (which drives rate limits and model tiers)?
  - **Geography and compliance.** Regions, data residency, retention, training-data opt-out, abuse and CSAM obligations.
#### Back-of-envelope
  - 1M DAU x about 20 messages/day = **20M messages/day**, about 230/s on average. With a 3x diurnal peak plus event spikes, design for **\~750/s with 2x burst headroom**.
  - Output: about 400 tokens per response, so **8B output tokens/day**, \~280k tok/s at peak.
  - Input: with multi-turn history the average context is about 3K tokens, or 60B input tokens/day. **With prefix caching (system prompt plus prior turns), 70-90% of input KV is reusable** if requests are routed back to the replica holding the conversation's cache.
  - **Concurrent streams:** 750/s x \~12 s ≈ 9k SSE connections, which a handful of gateway pods can hold.
  - **Storage:** 20M messages x \~2 KB ≈ 40 GB/day of text, \~15 TB/year before replication. Attachments are separate, in object storage.
  - **GPUs** (a 70B-class FP8 model, \~5k output tok/s per 4xH100 replica at the TPOT SLO): 280k / 5k ≈ 56 replicas for decode, plus prefill (mostly cached), plus 30% headroom, giving **\~80 replicas ≈ 320 H100s**. At \~$2.50/GPU-hr that is about **$19k/day, roughly $0.02 per DAU per day**. Routing 60% of traffic to an 8B-class model cuts this about 2x. Inference is about 80-90% of total cost, so routing and caching are the biggest levers.
#### Architecture
1.  **Edge.** CDN/WAF, then a global load balancer, then the regional API. Authentication, per-user and per-tier **token-bucket rate limits** (messages/min plus tokens/day), and bot and abuse detection.
2.  **Chat API (stateless).** POST /conversations/{id}/messages (with an idempotency key), then GET .../stream (SSE with a resumable stream log, as in Q22). Conversation CRUD, titles, search and share links.
3.  **Conversation store.**
      - Messages are partitioned by conversation\_id and clustered by time (Cassandra/Scylla, DynamoDB or sharded Postgres/Vitess).
      - A user index lists conversations by user\_id. Messages are append-only with branching (edit/regenerate creates a parent\_message\_id tree).
      - A Redis hot cache holds recent conversations.
      - Search uses async indexing into OpenSearch or a vector store.
4.  **Orchestrator.** Loads history, then **context management**: keep the last N turns verbatim plus a rolling summary when over budget (for example a 32K budget against a 128K model limit, for cost). Retrieval and memory, tool or web-search calls, and a model router that classifies difficulty and tier. Input and output **safety classifiers** run in parallel with generation; output moderation runs over streamed chunks with the ability to cut the stream.
5.  **Inference gateway.** Picks the model pool and the replica using **conversation-affinity routing** (consistent hash on conversation\_id with bounded load) to hit the prefix cache. Handles priority queues (paid \> free, interactive \> batch), deadlines and cancellation propagation.
6.  **Inference fleet.** vLLM/SGLang-class engines with continuous batching, chunked prefill and FP8 (Q21), in multiple regions. Autoscale on queue depth and KV utilisation. Pre-warmed capacity is needed because cold start takes minutes.
7.  **Async pipeline.** Usage metering, title generation and summarisation (batch, low priority, off-peak GPUs), analytics, feedback, eval sampling, training-data pipelines that respect opt-outs.
8.  **Observability.** Traces per message (Q23), with prompt and model versions on every request. Rollout and rollback through a prompt and model registry (Q24).
#### Degradation and reliability
  - **Overload ladder:**
    1.  Queue with a deadline.
    2.  Shed free tier first.
    3.  Route to a smaller model.
    4.  Cap max output tokens.
    5.  Return "at capacity" with a retry-after. Never let queues grow unboundedly; TTFT collapses for everyone.
  - **Multi-region active-active** for the API. GPU pools can spill over across regions (with a latency penalty). The conversation store is replicated, with the home region per user.
  - **Failure isolation.** A bad replica (NaNs, a stuck NCCL collective) is detected by health probes plus output sanity checks and drained automatically.
#### How to measure success
  - **User-facing:** TTFT p50/p95/p99, tokens/s, error and "stopped/truncated" rate, regenerate and thumbs-down rate, D1/D7 retention.
  - **Efficiency:** GPU-hours per 1k messages, prefix-cache hit rate, goodput, cost per DAU, router accuracy (quality held while the share of traffic on the small model rises).
  - **Safety:** moderation precision and recall, abuse rate, time to mitigate jailbreak waves.
### Tradeoffs
|  |  |  |  |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **Option** | **Pros** | **Cons** | **When to choose** |
| Vendor model API | No GPU ops; frontier quality; fast launch | Per-token cost at scale; rate limits; less control | Early stage; \<\~$1M/yr inference, or when quality is the moat |
| Self-hosted open model | 3-10x cheaper at steady high volume; control over latency and data | GPU capacity planning, on-call, quality gap | Sustained volume, predictable load, data constraints |
| Single model | Simple, consistent behaviour | Overpays on easy queries | Small scale, premium product |
| Tiered model router | 2-4x cost reduction | Misroutes hurt quality; harder to evaluate | 1M+ DAU with a mixed-difficulty workload |
| Wide-column store (Cassandra/Dynamo) | Linear write scaling; simple partitioning | Weak ad-hoc queries; needs a separate search index | Huge append-only message volume |
| Sharded Postgres/Vitess | Transactions, flexible queries, familiar | Resharding operations | Moderate scale, richer relational features |

  - **Context length vs cost.** Every extra 1K tokens of history is paid on every turn (O(n²) over a conversation without caching). Summarisation and cache affinity are core design elements, not optimisations.
  - **Run safety checks in parallel vs in series.** In parallel saves \~100-300 ms TTFT, but you may stream tokens you then retract.
### Pitfalls interviewers probe
  - **Designing the CRUD for 20 minutes and hand-waving inference.** The GPUs carry \~85% of the cost and set the latency.
  - **No back-of-envelope.** You can't defend capacity, cost per DAU or the number of GPUs without one.
  - **Ignoring multi-turn context growth and prefix caching**: random load balancing destroys cache hit rate and doubles prefill cost.
  - **No overload strategy.** A launch or viral spike produces unbounded queues and timeouts, and retries amplify the load.
  - **Forgetting abuse.** Scripted free-tier scraping, jailbreak waves and prompt injection via uploads need rate limits, classifiers and per-user token budgets.
  - **Weak data lifecycle.** Deletion requests must cascade to caches, search indexes, stream logs, backups and training sets, and training opt-out must be respected.
